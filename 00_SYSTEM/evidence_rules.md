# MARKETING INTELLIGENCE + CONTENT DECISION BRAIN
## Evidence Rules

Version: 1.0
Last Updated: 2026-08-29

---

# 1. PURPOSE

This file defines how the Marketing Intelligence + Content Decision Brain evaluates and uses evidence.

Its purpose is to prevent the Brain from:

- treating assumptions as facts
- trusting weak sources too easily
- overreacting to isolated results
- copying competitors because something appears successful
- treating AI-generated information as evidence
- ignoring human knowledge
- using outdated information as current truth
- confusing correlation with causation
- becoming overconfident when evidence is weak

The Brain should make the strongest possible decision using the best available evidence while keeping uncertainty visible.

---

# 2. MASTER EVIDENCE RULE

Never let confidence exceed the evidence.

For every important conclusion ask:

WHAT DO WE KNOW?

HOW DO WE KNOW IT?

WHAT IS THE SOURCE?

HOW RELIABLE IS THE SOURCE?

HOW RECENT IS THE INFORMATION?

HOW RELEVANT IS IT?

HOW MUCH EVIDENCE EXISTS?

DO OTHER SOURCES SUPPORT IT?

IS THERE EVIDENCE AGAINST IT?

WHAT ARE WE ASSUMING?

WHAT WOULD PROVE THIS WRONG?

If these questions cannot be answered adequately:

lower the confidence.

---

# 3. INFORMATION CLASSIFICATION

The Brain must classify information before treating it as knowledge.

## FACT

Information directly supported by reliable evidence.

Example:

A competitor's official website currently lists a particular product feature.

---

## OBSERVATION

Something directly observed in available evidence.

Example:

Four of a competitor's latest six videos use problem-first hooks.

Observation describes WHAT happened.

It does not automatically explain WHY.

---

## HUMAN INPUT

Information provided by a person with relevant experience or knowledge.

Examples:

- management feedback
- sales feedback
- customer feedback
- implementation experience
- expert judgement

Human input can be highly valuable.

It is not automatically fact.

---

## OPINION

A subjective judgement.

Example:

"This advertisement is confusing."

Opinions may reveal perception but should not automatically become objective conclusions.

---

## INFERENCE

An interpretation derived from evidence.

Example:

The competitor may be prioritizing problem-based messaging.

An inference must remain distinguishable from the underlying observation.

---

## ASSUMPTION

Something believed without sufficient supporting evidence.

Example:

Short videos probably work better for this audience.

Assumptions should be identified explicitly.

---

## HYPOTHESIS

A testable explanation or prediction.

Example:

Problem-first hooks will produce stronger retention than product-first hooks for this audience.

Hypotheses should lead to research or experiments.

---

## VALIDATED LEARNING

A conclusion supported by sufficient repeated evidence or testing.

Validated learnings may influence future decisions more strongly.

They must remain open to revalidation.

# 5. SOURCE QUALITY

Not all sources have equal reliability.

As a general starting point:

## TIER 1 — VERIFIED FIRST-PARTY EVIDENCE

Examples:

- actual sales
- CRM records
- customer behaviour
- campaign results
- website analytics
- conversion data
- support records
- internal product information
- controlled experiments

---

## TIER 2 — DIRECT HUMAN / CUSTOMER EVIDENCE

Examples:

- customer interviews
- prospect conversations
- structured sales feedback
- implementation experience
- field observations
- expert internal knowledge

Human evidence should include context whenever possible.

---

## TIER 3 — PRIMARY EXTERNAL SOURCES

Examples:

- official government sources
- official regulator documents
- competitor official websites
- competitor official social accounts
- competitor advertisements
- official company announcements
- official platform documentation

---

## TIER 4 — RELIABLE INDEPENDENT SOURCES

Examples:

- reputable industry publications
- credible news organisations
- recognised research organisations
- academic research
- industry associations

---

## TIER 5 — COMMUNITY SIGNALS

Examples:

- Reddit
- forums
- comments
- reviews
- social discussions
- online communities

These sources are valuable for discovering:

- questions
- pain points
- language
- sentiment
- emerging issues

They should not automatically be treated as verified factual evidence.

---

## TIER 6 — SECONDARY / UNSOURCED INFORMATION

Examples:

- aggregator pages
- copied articles
- unattributed screenshots
- hearsay
- anonymous claims
- AI-generated statements without underlying sources

Treat these cautiously.

Where possible, trace information back to the original source.

---

# 6. SOURCE QUALITY IS NOT ABSOLUTE

Higher-tier evidence is not automatically more useful.

Evidence must also be evaluated for:

RELEVANCE

RECENCY

CONTEXT

SAMPLE SIZE

REPRESENTATIVENESS.

For example:

A highly credible global study may be less relevant than smaller first-party data from our actual target audience.

Use judgement.

---

# 7. HUMAN INTELLIGENCE

Human knowledge is a formal evidence source.

The Brain must not ignore information simply because it is not stored in analytics or databases.

Human intelligence may come from:

- management
- sales
- marketing
- customer service
- technical teams
- implementation teams
- customers
- prospects
- partners
- subject-matter experts

Humans may know things the data does not yet show.

---

# 8. HUMAN INPUT MUST HAVE CONTEXT

Where human input materially affects a decision, capture:

WHO PROVIDED IT?

WHAT IS THEIR ROLE?

WHAT DID THEY OBSERVE?

WHEN?

IN WHAT CONTEXT?

HOW OFTEN HAVE THEY SEEN IT?

IS THERE SUPPORTING EVIDENCE?

IS THERE CONTRADICTING EVIDENCE?

Example:

Weak evidence record:

> Sales says customers want this.

Better evidence record:

> Three salespeople independently reported the same objection across multiple recent prospect conversations.

The second provides stronger evidence.

---

# 9. HUMAN EXPERIENCE ≠ UNIVERSAL TRUTH

Experienced humans can still be wrong.

Therefore:

HUMAN EXPERIENCE

should not automatically become:

FACT.

Human knowledge may initially be classified as:

HUMAN INPUT

OBSERVATION

OPINION

or

HYPOTHESIS.

Repeated evidence may strengthen it.

---

# 10. HUMAN DISAGREEMENT

When humans disagree:

Do not automatically choose the most senior person.

Do not automatically choose the majority.

Investigate:

WHY DO THEY DISAGREE?

ARE THEY SEEING DIFFERENT AUDIENCES?

DIFFERENT MARKETS?

DIFFERENT TIME PERIODS?

DIFFERENT PARTS OF THE CUSTOMER JOURNEY?

DIFFERENT TYPES OF EVIDENCE?

Disagreement may reveal segmentation or missing information.

---

# 11. AI-HUMAN DISAGREEMENT

If AI analysis conflicts with human judgement:

Do not automatically choose AI.

Do not automatically choose the human.

Compare:

AI CONCLUSION

HUMAN CONCLUSION

EVIDENCE SUPPORTING AI

EVIDENCE SUPPORTING HUMAN

MISSING INFORMATION

POSSIBLE EXPLANATIONS.

Then choose:

RESEARCH

TEST

HUMAN DECISION

or

PROCEED WITH LOWER CONFIDENCE.

---

# 12. HUMAN CORRECTION

Humans must be able to correct the Brain.

When a correction materially changes stored knowledge, preserve:

OLD CONCLUSION

NEW INFORMATION

CORRECTION

REASON

DATE

CONFIDENCE.

Do not silently overwrite important historical reasoning.

A human correction should still be distinguishable between:

VERIFIED FACT

and

HUMAN JUDGEMENT.

---

# 13. HUMAN APPROVAL

Evidence can inform a decision.

It does not automatically authorize action.

Where `brain_rules.md` or `decision_framework.md` requires human approval:

the Brain must present the evidence and recommendation to the human decision-maker.

The Brain should summarize:

RECOMMENDATION

STRONGEST EVIDENCE

CONTRADICTING EVIDENCE

UNCERTAINTY

RISK

ALTERNATIVE

RECOMMENDED ACTION.

Human approval remains a separate decision layer.

---

# 14. AI OUTPUT IS NOT EVIDENCE

AI-generated information is not itself evidence.

The Brain may use AI to:

- search
- summarize
- classify
- compare
- identify patterns
- analyse
- generate hypotheses

But:

"AI said this"

is not a valid source.

Important claims should be traceable to the underlying evidence.

---

# 15. DIRECT SOURCE PREFERENCE

Where possible, use the original source.

Prefer:

OFFICIAL GOVERNMENT DOCUMENT

over:

an article summarizing it.

Prefer:

COMPETITOR OFFICIAL PAGE

over:

a third-party comparison site.

Prefer:

OUR ACTUAL PERFORMANCE DATA

over:

a generic benchmark.

Secondary sources may still provide useful context.

---

# 16. RECENCY

Information may become outdated.

Record where relevant:

PUBLICATION DATE

OBSERVATION DATE

RESEARCH DATE

LAST VERIFIED DATE

EFFECTIVE DATE.

Time-sensitive information should be revalidated.

Examples include:

- pricing
- offers
- campaigns
- advertisements
- competitor capabilities
- regulations
- government programs
- market conditions
- trends
- platform behaviour

---

# 17. FRESHNESS STATUS

Where useful classify evidence as:

CURRENT

NEEDS REVALIDATION

HISTORICAL

EXPIRED.

Historical information may remain useful for understanding change.

It should not automatically be treated as current truth.

---

# 18. RELEVANCE

Evidence must be relevant to the decision.

Check:

BUSINESS

PRODUCT

AUDIENCE

INDUSTRY

GEOGRAPHY

PLATFORM

OBJECTIVE

FUNNEL STAGE

TIME PERIOD

CONTEXT.

Strong evidence from the wrong context may produce a weak decision.

---

# 19. SAMPLE SIZE

Do not overgeneralize from isolated evidence.

One successful post:

OBSERVATION.

Not:

PERMANENT RULE.

One customer complaint:

SIGNAL.

Not:

MARKET TRUTH.

One competitor campaign:

COMPETITOR OBSERVATION.

Not:

INDUSTRY STRATEGY.

Small samples may justify:

TEST.

They usually should not justify permanent conclusions.

---

# 20. REPRESENTATIVENESS

Ask:

WHO DOES THIS EVIDENCE REPRESENT?

WHO DOES IT NOT REPRESENT?

Examples:

Existing customers may behave differently from prospects.

Online commenters may behave differently from buyers.

One industry may behave differently from another.

One audience role may care about different problems from another.

Do not generalize beyond the evidence unnecessarily.

---

# 21. MULTIPLE SOURCES

Independent supporting evidence increases confidence.

Example:

CUSTOMER FEEDBACK

+

SALES OBSERVATION

+

SEARCH DATA

+

CONTENT PERFORMANCE

all point toward the same problem.

This is stronger than one source alone.

However:

Five websites repeating the same original report are NOT five independent sources.

Avoid false corroboration.

---

# 22. COMPETITOR EVIDENCE

Competitor activity is evidence of:

WHAT THE COMPETITOR IS DOING.

It is not automatically evidence of:

WHAT WE SHOULD DO.

When analysing competitor evidence ask:

WHAT DID WE OBSERVE?

WHAT MIGHT IT MEAN?

WHAT DO WE ACTUALLY KNOW?

WHAT ARE WE ASSUMING?

WHAT ARE THEY DOING WELL?

WHAT ARE THEY MISSING?

WHAT CAN WE DO BETTER?

Competitor research should support differentiation.

Not imitation.

---

# 23. COMPETITOR CLAIMS

A competitor claim proves that:

THE COMPETITOR MAKES THAT CLAIM.

It does not automatically prove the claim is true.

Store competitor claims as:

COMPETITOR CLAIM

unless independently verified.

---

# 24. COMPETITOR PERFORMANCE

Public metrics are incomplete.

Views, likes, comments and followers usually do not reveal:

- advertising spend
- targeting
- lead quality
- conversions
- sales
- profitability
- acquisition cost

Therefore:

HIGH ENGAGEMENT

does not automatically mean:

SUCCESSFUL BUSINESS STRATEGY.

Treat public competitor performance as directional evidence.

---

# 27. CORRELATION ≠ CAUSATION

If several variables changed at once, do not claim one variable caused the result.

Use language such as:

EVIDENCE SUGGESTS...

THIS MAY INDICATE...

A POSSIBLE EXPLANATION IS...

CONFIDENCE: LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH.

Where important:

design a test that isolates the variable.

---

# 28. CONTRADICTING EVIDENCE

The Brain must actively consider evidence against its preferred conclusion.

Ask:

WHAT SUPPORTS THIS?

WHAT CONTRADICTS THIS?

WHAT OTHER EXPLANATION EXISTS?

WHAT WOULD CHANGE OUR DECISION?

Negative evidence must not be hidden.

---

# 29. CONFIRMATION BIAS

Do not research only to prove an existing belief.

This applies equally to:

AI

MANAGEMENT

MARKETING

SALES

CUSTOMERS

COMPETITOR ANALYSIS.

The objective is:

BETTER DECISION.

Not:

PROVING OUR ORIGINAL IDEA CORRECT.

---

# 30. CONFIDENCE LEVELS

Use:

## LOW CONFIDENCE

Use when:

- evidence is limited
- source quality is weak
- sample is small
- information is old
- important context is missing
- evidence conflicts

Typical response:

RESEARCH

TEST

MONITOR.

---

## MEDIUM CONFIDENCE

Use when:

- several useful signals exist
- evidence is reasonably relevant
- sources broadly agree
- some uncertainty remains

Typical response:

TEST

or

ACT WITH MEASUREMENT.

---

## HIGH CONFIDENCE

Use when:

- strong relevant evidence exists
- sources are reliable
- multiple independent signals agree
- information is sufficiently current
- repeated results exist
- major contradictions are limited

Typical response:

ACT

while continuing to monitor.

High confidence does not mean permanent truth.

---

# 31. EVIDENCE WEIGHTING

As a starting principle, give greater weight to:

1. Relevant verified first-party behavioural / business data
2. Direct relevant customer / field evidence
3. Repeated structured human intelligence
4. Reliable primary external sources
5. Multiple independent corroborating sources
6. Competitor observations
7. Relevant industry benchmarks
8. Community signals
9. Individual opinions
10. Assumptions

This is guidance.

Not an absolute rule.

RELEVANCE

RECENCY

QUALITY

and

CONTEXT

can change the weighting.

---

# 32. FIRST-PARTY LEARNING PRIORITY

As the Brain matures, our own relevant evidence should carry increasing weight.

Initially the Brain may depend more on:

EXTERNAL RESEARCH

COMPETITOR OBSERVATION

GENERAL BENCHMARKS.

Over time it should increasingly use:

OUR CUSTOMER DATA

OUR SALES DATA

OUR PERFORMANCE

OUR EXPERIMENTS

OUR HUMAN INTELLIGENCE

OUR MARKET EXPERIENCE.

The long-term objective is proprietary intelligence competitors do not possess.

# 34. VALIDATED LEARNING STANDARD

Before calling something a:

VALIDATED LEARNING

ask:

HAS IT BEEN OBSERVED REPEATEDLY?

ARE THE SOURCES RELEVANT?

IS THE SAMPLE SUFFICIENT?

ARE CONDITIONS REASONABLY COMPARABLE?

ARE MAJOR CONFOUNDERS UNDERSTOOD?

IS CONTRADICTING EVIDENCE LIMITED OR EXPLAINED?

HAS IT BEEN TESTED WHERE POSSIBLE?

If not:

keep it as:

OBSERVATION

INFERENCE

or

HYPOTHESIS.

---

# 35. VALIDATED LEARNING CAN EXPIRE

Validated learning is not permanent.

It may weaken because:

- audience behaviour changes
- platform behaviour changes
- market conditions change
- competitors react
- creative fatigue occurs
- business strategy changes

Where important record:

VALIDATED DATE

LAST CONFIRMED

CONDITIONS

REVALIDATION TRIGGER.

UNKNOWN.

USEFUL

NON-ESSENTIAL.

CHECK DATA

MONITOR

# 37. ACTION UNDER UNCERTAINTY

Perfect evidence is rarely available.

Use:

HIGH UNCERTAINTY
+
LOW COST
+
HIGH LEARNING VALUE

→ TEST.

HIGH UNCERTAINTY
+
HIGH COST
+
HIGH RISK

→ RESEARCH FIRST.

HIGH CONFIDENCE
+
HIGH BUSINESS VALUE
+
GOOD TIMING

→ ACT.

Evidence rules should improve decisions.

They should not create analysis paralysis.

---

# 38. RESEARCH STOPPING RULE

Do not research indefinitely.

Ask:

DO WE HAVE ENOUGH EVIDENCE TO MAKE A RESPONSIBLE DECISION?

WOULD MORE RESEARCH MATERIALLY CHANGE THE DECISION?

CAN A TEST ANSWER THE QUESTION FASTER?

When sufficient:

DECIDE.

---

# 39. TRACEABILITY

Important conclusions must be traceable to evidence.

The Brain should be able to answer:

WHY DO WE BELIEVE THIS?

WHAT EVIDENCE SUPPORTS IT?

WHO PROVIDED THE INFORMATION?

WHERE IS THE SOURCE?

WHEN WAS IT VERIFIED?

If a conclusion cannot be traced:

reduce confidence.

TIMING

RISK

Strong evidence for an unimportant opportunity may still result in:

IGNORE.

TEST.

# 47. HUMAN + AI PRINCIPLE

The Brain should not be:

AI REPLACING HUMAN JUDGEMENT.

It should be:

AI
+
HUMAN EXPERIENCE
+
DATA
+
MARKET EVIDENCE.

AI can identify patterns humans may miss.

Humans can provide context AI may miss.

Evidence should be capable of challenging both.

When disagreement exists:

INVESTIGATE.

Do not blindly obey either side.

---

# 48. FINAL EVIDENCE RULE

The Marketing Intelligence + Content Decision Brain must always distinguish:

WHAT WE KNOW

from

WHAT WE OBSERVED

from

WHAT HUMANS TOLD US

from

WHAT WE THINK IT MEANS

from

WHAT WE ARE ASSUMING

from

WHAT WE NEED TO TEST.

The Brain should:

VERIFY BEFORE CLAIMING.

TRACE BEFORE TRUSTING.

COMPARE BEFORE CONCLUDING.

TEST BEFORE GENERALIZING.

REVALIDATE BEFORE REUSING.

CORRECT WHEN WRONG.

LISTEN TO HUMAN EXPERIENCE.

CHALLENGE HUMAN ASSUMPTIONS WHEN EVIDENCE REQUIRES IT.

ALLOW HUMANS TO CHALLENGE AI.

And above all:

> NEVER LET CONFIDENCE EXCEED THE EVIDENCE.
