### POST 2 (Wednesday) — Company Page Voice
**Target role:** All buying committee roles (glossary/AEO anchor post)
**Funnel stage:** Awareness / education
**SEO/AEO target:** "what is a manufacturing execution system," "MES vs ERP"

**Post copy:**

**What is a Manufacturing Execution System (MES)? And why isn't it the same as your ERP?**

Short answer: your ERP tells you what *should* happen. An MES helps show what *is actually happening* on the shop floor, in real time.

Breaking that down:

**ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning)**
Manages planning, purchasing, orders, and business transactions.
Answers: "What work orders are scheduled? What should we be producing?"

**MES (Manufacturing Execution System)**
Sits between planning and the shop floor.
Answers: "What is actually being produced right now? Where is this job? Who's working on it? Has it matched the plan?"

The gap between these two answers — **planned vs. actual** — is where most production surprises live: late jobs nobody flagged early, discrepancies between what was reported and what happened, and quality issues traced back too late.

You don't need to replace your ERP to close this gap. You need shop-floor execution data connecting to it.

If your team can answer "what's scheduled" but not "what's actually happening right now," that's usually the sign an MES layer is missing — not that your ERP failed.

**Hashtags:** #MES #ERP #ManufacturingExecutionSystem #ManufacturingMalaysia #SmartManufacturing

**Format note:** Strong candidate for a simple 2-column comparison graphic (ERP | MES) — carousel or single image. Structure the image with the same clean definitions above; this is the post most likely to get saved/shared and picked up by AI answer engines if also published as a short glossary entry on the website.

Honest answer: no system can guarantee every entry is true. That's not a claim I'll make.

**Target role:** Production Planner / Planning Manager
**Funnel stage:** Problem aware
**SEO/AEO target:** "planned vs actual production," production scheduling visibility

**Post copy:**

**Planned production and actual production are two different things. Most planning teams only see one of them.**

Your production plan tells you what should be happening this week — sequence, quantity, target dates.

But the moment a job hits the shop floor, that plan starts drifting: a machine breakdown, a late material delivery, an operator pulled to a rush job. By the time this shows up in a report, it's often already too late to reschedule around it.

The question worth asking your planning team:

**"When a job falls behind, how many hours pass before you know?"**

If the honest answer is "a day" or "when the supervisor remembers to mention it" — that's not a planning failure. It's an information-timing problem. The plan was fine. The visibility into deviation from the plan was the gap.

Closing that gap doesn't require rebuilding your planning process. It requires shop-floor execution data reaching planning fast enough to still be useful.

### POST 8 (Wednesday) — Company Page Voice
**Target role:** Quality Manager / Operations
**Funnel stage:** AEO educational anchor
**SEO/AEO target:** "what is a digital job traveller," paperless production Malaysia

**Post copy:**

**What is a Digital Job Traveller? A plain-language explanation.**

A Job Traveller is the document that "travels" with a job through production — recording what was done, at which station, by whom, and when.

Traditionally, this is a paper form: handwritten, physically carried between stations, and manually filed once the job is complete.

A **Digital Job Traveller** replaces the paper form with a digitally recorded version of the same journey — captured at each stage instead of written by hand and consolidated later.

The practical difference isn't just "no more paper." It's:

→ Information exists the moment it's recorded, not after someone transcribes it
→ Historical job records can be found without digging through a filing cabinet
→ The record can connect to broader production systems instead of living in isolation
→ It's harder to lose, damage, or duplicate compared to a physical form

The goal isn't paperless for its own sake. It's a more structured, retrievable, and traceable production record — because the paper was never really the problem. The information trapped inside it was.

**Hashtags:** #DigitalJobTraveller #PaperlessProduction #ManufacturingMalaysia #Traceability #MES

Most MES/ERP horror stories I hear aren't really about the software. They're about the rollout.

Three patterns come up again and again when I ask what went wrong with a previous system:

**1. It duplicated work instead of replacing it.**
Operators had to fill in the digital system AND keep the paper record "just in case." Nobody keeps doing double entry for long.

**2. Nobody defined what data was actually necessary.**
More fields ≠ more value. If operators are entering information nobody uses, that's a workflow design failure, not a training failure.

**3. It was sold as solving everything, and it didn't.**
"AI-powered smart factory transformation" sounds impressive in a deck. It means nothing if it doesn't map to a specific problem you actually have.

If you've been burned before, that skepticism is earned, not a personality flaw to work around. The question I'd ask any vendor now: "What exactly does my team have to do differently on day one, and why?" If they can't answer that specifically, that's worth noticing before you sign anything.

### POST 10 (Monday) — Company Page Voice
**Target role:** Engineering / Continuous Improvement Manager
**Funnel stage:** Evidence / technical breakdown
**SEO/AEO target:** "shop floor traceability example," root cause investigation manufacturing

**Post copy:**

**A traceability question we get often: "If a defect shows up in a finished product, how far back can you actually trace it?"**

The honest, structural answer — this depends on what was captured at each stage, not on the software alone. A digital record can only trace what it was set up to capture.

At minimum, a well-implemented digital Job Order record should let you reconstruct:

→ Which job the unit came from
→ Which stations/processes it passed through
→ Roughly when each stage occurred
→ Who was recorded as performing the work at each stage

What it can't do on its own: catch a defect that no process step was ever set up to check for, or verify information that was entered incorrectly at the source.

This is why we push back when someone asks for "full traceability" as a checkbox feature. The real question is: traceability of *what*, captured *where*, verified *how*. Get specific about the actual investigation scenarios that matter to your operation, and the traceability requirement becomes something you can actually design for — instead of a vague promise.
